How to Choose Your First Process to Automate

A guide to choosing a business process, understanding its exceptions, and measuring an initial automation before rolling it out to the team.

Weaves Team · 2026-11-23 · 2 min read · Updated 2026-11-24

In this article

  1. Pick a specific task
  2. Map inputs, decisions, and exceptions
  3. Plan a small test
  4. Plan for maintenance
  5. Frequently asked questions

Quick summary

  • Choose a specific, recurring process with a designated owner.
  • Document inputs, decisions, systems, and exceptions before you build.
  • Compare how the process works before and after, keeping activity separate from outcomes.

Choosing a tool before understanding the work often moves the same problems to a new screen. A useful first automation starts with a simpler question. Which recurring task interrupts the team, and what should happen when something falls outside the usual process?

Pick a specific task

Narrow a broad goal like “automate administration” to a recognizable sequence, such as receiving a request, checking some information, and notifying the person responsible. Ask the people who do the work to explain how it starts and how they know it's finished.

Identify who owns the process and check that the rules recur often enough to document. If every case requires a different negotiation, the automation may work best as a tool that supports the team's judgment.

Map inputs, decisions, and exceptions

Write down where information comes from, where it's stored, and what decisions people make. Include the harder cases, such as incomplete data, duplicates, last-minute changes, or a tool that stops responding.

For each exception, decide who gets notified and how the work continues. An automation that fails silently can create more review work than the original manual task.

Plan a small test

Keep the scope small and agree with the team on what to track, such as time spent, completed requests, issues, or response time, depending on the process. Record a baseline and review the results during the test.

The number of completed tasks measures activity. Assess savings or service improvements with comparable data, and ask the people using the workflow whether the process is clearer now.

Plan for maintenance

Decide who updates the rules, reviews errors, and approves changes. Check permissions, how information is handled, and how to return to the manual procedure if necessary.

Once the test works and the team can run it, decide whether it's worth expanding. Choose the next automation based on a need you've identified. The availability of a new tool alone is insufficient.

Frequently asked questions

Do you need to replace all your tools?

Not necessarily. First check which systems you use and what integrations they support. Then decide whether any need to change.

What happens when there's an exception?

Identify exceptions in the design, assign someone to handle them, and specify how to resume work if the automation stops.
